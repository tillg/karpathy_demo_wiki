# Knot theory (Wikipedia)

*Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_theory (accessed 2026-10-02). Summarised in own words.*

A mathematical **knot** is like a knotted piece of string whose two ends have been glued together, so it can never
be untied without cutting. Formally it is an embedding of a circle in three-dimensional Euclidean space. The plain
ring is the **unknot**; the simplest genuinely knotted example is the **trefoil**.

**When are two knots the same?** When one can be moved into the other by an *ambient isotopy* — a continuous
deformation of the surrounding space that never lets the string pass through itself.

**Knot diagrams and Reidemeister moves.** Knots are usually drawn as flat diagrams with over- and under-crossings.
In 1927 Alexander and Briggs, and independently Reidemeister, showed that two diagrams describe the same knot exactly
when one can be turned into the other by a sequence of three kinds of local moves — the **Reidemeister moves**
(adding or removing a twist, sliding one strand over another, and moving a strand past a crossing).

**Knot invariants.** Because pictures are deceptive, knots are told apart by invariants — quantities that come out
the same for every diagram of a knot:

- *crossing number* — the fewest crossings any diagram of the knot can have;
- *Alexander polynomial* (1928);
- *Jones polynomial* (1984, Vaughan Jones), a stronger tool.

**History.** In the 1860s Lord Kelvin suggested atoms might be knotted vortices in the aether. The idea was wrong,
but it led Peter Guthrie Tait to compile the first knot tables.

**Applications.** Knot theory helps understand how enzymes act on DNA, chirality of molecules in chemistry, and
ideas for topological quantum computing.

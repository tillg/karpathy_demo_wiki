# Honey process coffee (Seattle Coffee Gear)

*Source: https://www.seattlecoffeegear.com/blogs/learning-center/honey-process-coffee (accessed 2026-10-02). Summarised in own words.*

A guide to the three main ways of turning ripe coffee cherries into dry green beans, with a focus on the honey
process.

**The three routes:**

- *Washed:* the skin and fruit are removed mechanically and the bean is dried without any fruit layer on it.
- *Natural:* the whole cherry is dried intact, with all fruit layers still around the seed.
- *Honey:* the skin is removed, but some or all of the sticky mucilage layer is deliberately left on the bean while it
  dries. The tacky coating is what gave the method its name — there is no honey involved.

**Colours of honey.** Producers label honey coffees by how much mucilage stays on:

- *Yellow honey:* most of the mucilage removed, only a thin coat left. Clean and sweet, closest to a washed coffee.
- *Red honey:* a moderate amount left. More fruit-forward and syrupy, with more body.
- *Black honey:* nearly all mucilage kept, and it dries the longest. Rich and jammy, closer to a natural.

**Origin.** The method was developed in Costa Rica in the early 2000s, as a middle way between the clean brightness of
washed coffee and the sweetness of naturals. It has spread through Central America, with Costa Rica still the
benchmark.

**Cup.** Compared with washed coffees, honeys are usually sweeter and rounder, with more body and softer acidity —
stone fruit, caramel, brown sugar. Darker honeys approach a natural's sweetness without as much fermented flavour.

**Risk.** Black honey in particular needs close watching during drying: frequent turning and temperature control to
avoid mould and over-fermentation.
